Building in public: lessons from a Nigerian developer

What two years of freelancing across healthcare, government, and social impact taught me about shipping software that actually matters.

CareerFreelancingNigeria

Two years ago I took my first paid client project — a small business website. Today I've built a government intelligence platform, an enterprise ERP, a telehealth tool for community health workers, and a handful of other things in between. None of them were easy. All of them were educational in ways I didn't anticipate.

Here are the things I'd tell myself at the start.

Scope is the enemy

Every project I've delivered has been larger than the original brief. Not because clients are dishonest — they genuinely don't know what they need until you start building it. The only protection is to define scope in writing before writing a single line of code, and to make every addition a formal conversation, not a favour.

"Can you just add…" is the most expensive sentence in software development.

Build for the actual user, not the person paying

On the NTAL telehealth project, the paying stakeholder wanted a polished mobile app. The actual users — community health workers in rural Nigeria — had feature phones and patchy mobile data. Those are different products. I pushed back and we built for USSD first. The app exists, but the USSD version is what actually gets used.

Know who will open your software at 7am in a village with no internet. Build for them.

Documentation is a product

Code that isn't documented eventually becomes code that can't be maintained. I learned this the hard way on the CrownJewel ERP — 18 months of feature additions with no systemic docs meant the sixth month of a new module required archaeology rather than engineering.

I now write a short architecture note at the start of every module: what it does, what it depends on, what the main data flows are. It takes 30 minutes and saves hours later.

Pricing is communication

Cheap work attracts clients who don't value the work. I've done both — underpriced projects where the client treated me like an on-call employee, and appropriately priced projects where they treated the engagement seriously. The quality of the relationship correlates almost perfectly with the price.

Charge what the work is worth. It's not about money; it's about setting expectations on both sides.

The infrastructure problem in Nigeria is real and solvable

Unreliable electricity, inconsistent internet, expensive cloud hosting — these are constraints, not excuses. They push you toward specific architectural choices: offline-first, lightweight, USSD-compatible, aggressive caching. Working within these constraints makes you a better engineer, not a worse one.

The best software I've built has been better because of the environment it runs in, not in spite of it.

I'm still figuring this out. But I'm shipping, and that matters.
